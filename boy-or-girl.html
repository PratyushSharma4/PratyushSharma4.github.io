Probability and information

Boy or girl?

Core insight

The number you get is not a property of the family. It is a property of the process that handed you the fact, and two innocent-sounding phrasings encode two different processes.

Question

A family has two children. Assume each child is independently a boy or a girl with probability \( 1/2 \), so the four equally likely families are \( BB \), \( BG \), \( GB \), \( GG \), where the order records the elder child first.

You are told that at least one of the children is a boy. What is the probability that both children are boys? The instinct is to say \( 1/2 \), since the other child is equally likely to be a boy or a girl. Yet the standard answer is \( 1/3 \). And if instead you are told the elder child is a boy, the answer really is \( 1/2 \). How can one extra word change the probability?

Why the phrasing decides it

Start from the four equally likely families, each with prior probability \( 1/4 \).

\[ \{\, BB,\ BG,\ GB,\ GG \,\}. \]

"At least one is a boy." This statement is true for every family except \( GG \). It leaves three survivors, all still equally likely.

\[ \{\, BB,\ BG,\ GB \,\}, \qquad P(BB \mid \text{at least one boy}) = \frac{1}{3}. \]

"The elder child is a boy." This is a statement about a specific, labelled slot. It eliminates \( GB \) and \( GG \), leaving only two survivors.

\[ \{\, BB,\ BG \,\}, \qquad P(BB \mid \text{elder is a boy}) = \frac{1}{2}. \]

The extra word does not add a child. It adds an index. "At least one" ranges over an unlabelled pair and only rules out the all-girl case, so the two mixed families \( BG \) and \( GB \) both survive and outnumber \( BB \) two to one. "The elder" pins down a position, and conditioning on a labelled slot makes the other child a genuinely independent \( 1/2 \) coin.

Two tempting shortcuts lead people astray. The first collapses the two mixed families into a single "one of each" outcome and treats \( BB \), one of each, and \( GG \) as three equally likely cases. That is wrong. The mixed outcome carries two orderings, \( BG \) and \( GB \), so it is twice as likely as \( BB \), and the three cases are not equal. The second shortcut insists the answer must be \( 1/2 \) because the sex of one child is independent of the other. That independence is genuine before you are told anything. But "at least one is a boy" is a fact about the pair, and conditioning on it removes \( GG \) while sparing both mixed families, so the independence does not survive the filtering.

The hidden assumption in the naive \( 1/2 \) is that "you were told about a boy" is the same as "you inspected a fixed child and it was a boy." It is not. The answer is not encoded in the family at all. It is encoded in the rule by which the information reached you. This is why the puzzle is genuinely ambiguous when stated loosely. The phrase "one is a boy" does not say whether you asked does at least one boy exist? or is this particular child a boy?, and those two procedures return different numbers.
