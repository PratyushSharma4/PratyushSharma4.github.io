Number theory and data

Why real-world numbers start with 1

Core insight

If a rule about leading digits is to survive a change of units, it cannot treat the digits uniformly. It must be logarithmic, and the log scale makes 1 the widest bin.

Question

Collect the leading digit of a large pile of real-world numbers. Naively, each of the digits \( 1 \) through \( 9 \) should show up as the first digit about \( 1/9 \approx 11\% \) of the time. There is no obvious reason to prefer one over another.

Instead, the digit \( 1 \) appears first about \( 30\% \) of the time, and \( 9 \) barely \( 4.6\% \). This lopsided pattern is called Benford's law. It is so reliable that it is used to catch fabricated tax returns and rigged elections. Why would nature prefer numbers that begin with a \( 1 \)?

The explanation

The precise statement is that the probability of a leading digit \( d \) is

\[ P(d) = \log_{10}\!\left(1 + \frac{1}{d}\right), \qquad d \in \{1, 2, \ldots, 9\}. \]

The key is not the numbers themselves but the requirement of scale invariance. Switching the unit of measurement rescales every value by the same constant, yet it still describes the very same world. If there is any universal law governing leading digits, its predictions must not change when every value is multiplied by a constant. That single demand is enough to force the answer.

Consider where a number falls on a logarithmic axis. A number has leading digit \( 1 \) exactly when its fractional part on the \( \log_{10} \) scale lands between \( \log_{10} 1 = 0 \) and \( \log_{10} 2 \approx 0.301 \). It has leading digit \( 9 \) only when that fractional part lands between \( \log_{10} 9 \approx 0.954 \) and \( \log_{10} 10 = 1 \), a sliver of width \( \approx 0.046 \).

\[ P(d) = \log_{10}(d+1) - \log_{10}(d) = \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d+1}{d}\right). \]

Multiplying all the data by a constant simply shifts the fractional parts around the log scale by a fixed amount. A distribution that is unchanged by every possible shift on a circle of circumference \( 1 \) can only be the uniform distribution. So the fractional parts of the logarithms spread out evenly, and the probability of each leading digit is just the width of its bin on the log axis, which is exactly the formula above.

The intuition that all nine digits are equally likely quietly assumes the data lives on a linear scale, where the gap from \( 1 \) to \( 2 \) equals the gap from \( 8 \) to \( 9 \). Data that spans many orders of magnitude does not live there. It lives on the log scale, where \( 1 \) owns the widest doorway and \( 9 \) the narrowest.
